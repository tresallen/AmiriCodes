When I was a kid, one of my favorite shows was Code Name: Kids Next Door. A group of kids operation like a secret spy organization, taking on big mission with the wildest inventions and even wilder intentions. I used to think becoming a software engineer would feel like that. Go through school. Learn. Get the job. Get thrown a problem. Write the code. Fix it. Level up.

What I wasn’t prepared for was that, in today’s world, being just a software engineer on paper is not nearly enough. You have to become your own organization.

After my time at General Motors, I was sure I understood the industry. I had interned, and then fully experience production level enterprises. I had the cloud exposure. I worked with microservices. I became entrenched in full-stack work. I thought for sure I had REAL impact. But once I re-entered the job market, it became apparent, experience alone wouldn’t make me competitive, I needed more. Everything aspect of you had to be strong, and if one thing element was missing, your entire picture was weakened.

And mine wasn’t close to being strong enough.

### Purpose

I had to face the tough questions on the fly. Why am I doing this? Is it just for a job? Am I looking just to carve out a little career on the outside? Or am I here to master the craft?

I quickly realized the only way to survive was to ignore the sentiment of just wanting to be employed. I wanted the depth. I wanted the confidence to walk in to any room and belong. I wanted the feeling of accomplishment to be evident in the picture that I painted for every recruiter/hiring manager I encountered.

And so I went to work. My purpose stopped being just about holding on to a title. “Software Engineer.” It became about competence. The resume, the portfolio, the Github, the depth of understanding on all subjects, the articulation in interviews. All of these levels needed to be raised if I were to be the person I needed to be for myself.

### Pursuit

So I rebuilt everything. I rewrote my resume over 60 times. I restructured my projects to reflect architectural thinking. I studied system design and DSAs day and night. I built AI agents not because I was told to by a manager, but because it was paramount that I understood the frontier. The hunger in me had shifted. Now I was on the front foot, chasing skills, and leveling up.

### Process

This was the part that was hard to gauge initially. What worked in college no longer was adequate. The mountain I decided to climb had suddenly tripled in size, and so my habits had to change in order to match. Talent cannot compound without process. And so I created a routine; weekly DSA repetition. Deeper and deeper dives into distributed systems. Writing and rewriting technical breakdowns of all my past work and projects that I would eventually have to speak to. Iterating on how I would explain complexity. Tracking my weaknesses and critiquing every interview I got. I no longer feared the rejection: it became data points for progress. Every unsure answer and awkward silence to fill the void became a point of emphasis in my preparation. I finally understood what it meant to take the preparation seriously.

### Polish

Here lies the most shocking discovery I made. The work never stops. There’s always more to learn, the knowledge can always go deeper. The level only rises the further you go, and so you have to choose to either rise to the occasion or succumb to the noise and let the chips fall where they may. But being a victim of circumstance wasn’t something that I was interested in being again. And so we grind. We polish and we polish and we continue to rise because that is the only way we continue to have a say in where we land, the only way we keep some semblance of control on where we land next. And I am intent on shooting for the stars from here on out.
